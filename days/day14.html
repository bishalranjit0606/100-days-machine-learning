Day 14

How to Frame a Machine Learning Problem

Turn a business goal into a clear ML problem before you write any code.

233

The Netflix Business Problem

Today we learn how to convert a business problem into a machine learning problem.

Suppose you are a data scientist at Netflix. Leadership asks: how do we increase revenue?

Three business levers come to mind:

  • Marketing: bring more new subscribers.
  • Pricing: get existing customers to pay more.
  • Retention: stop existing customers from leaving.

All three can grow revenue. As data scientists we often focus on the third: keep people subscribed.

That is step one in framing. Start with the business goal. Then ask which part of it ML can help with.

234

From Revenue to Churn Rate

Instead of saying "increase revenue" in the meeting room, we narrow the goal to: decrease churn rate.

Churn rate is the share of subscribers who cancel in a period.

Example: Netflix has 100 subscribers and 2 leave. Churn rate is 2%.

In real life Netflix churn is around 4%. The team might set a target: bring it down to 3.75%.

That is a mathematical problem we can track. We leave the meeting with a clear number, not a vague wish.

Business problem: increase revenue. ML framing: reduce churn from 4% to 3.75%.

235

See the End Product First

Look at the big picture. What is the end product?

We do not need a model that predicts the churn rate number itself. Churn rate is a company level metric.

What we need: find if a customer is about to leave.

Once we know that, the product can act:

  • Send a 50% discount offer.
  • Fix a bad UI or help with no internet issues.
  • Reach out immediately when someone shows signs of leaving.

Good framing means you know what the model will do in production, not just what it will predict on paper.

236

Classification and Regression

Now we pick the type of ML problem.

Step 1: Is this person leaving or staying? That is a yes or no question.

Use supervised learning classification. Output: leave or stay.

Step 2: How strong is the feeling to leave? Example: 90% likely to cancel.

That is a number, not yes or no. Use regression to estimate willingness to leave.

Why both? A higher score can trigger a bigger discount. Someone at 90% might get more than someone at 40%.

A real churn solution often uses both models: classification to flag at risk users, regression to rank how urgent the case is.

237

Getting the Right Data

Getting data is one of the most important parts of framing.

Ask: what signals show that someone might leave? Examples:

  • Watch time dropping.
  • Search but did not find what they wanted.
  • Content left in the middle of a show.
  • Clicks on recommendations and the order they clicked.

You rarely pull this data alone. Talk to the data engineer.

They take live app data from OLTP systems and build a data warehouse where you can train models.

If the data you need does not exist yet, framing is not finished. No data means no model.

238

Define Your Metrics

Before you build, decide how you will measure success.

Metrics tell you if what you are doing is good or bad.

For churn you might track whether churn rate actually dropped after the model went live.

Pick metrics that tie back to the business goal. If leadership cares about revenue, show how the model affects revenue, not only model accuracy.

239

Online or Batch Learning

Another framing choice: will the model run in batch or online mode?

A common pipeline looks like this:

OLTP → data warehouse → model → trained model.

Batch learning: retrain on a schedule (daily or weekly). Good when patterns change slowly.

Online learning: update the model as new events arrive. Good when behavior shifts fast.

For churn, many teams start with batch: pull warehouse data, train overnight, score users the next day. Online can come later if you need faster reactions.

240

Check Your Assumptions

The last step in framing: check your assumptions before you invest months of work.

  • Is the data available? Can you actually get watch time, searches, and cancel labels?
  • Does one model fit all regions? Users in different countries may behave differently. You might need separate models by geography.
  • Can the product act on predictions? A discount offer only helps if the system can send it in time.

Catching a wrong assumption early saves the whole project.

241

Framing Steps Recap

Day 9 introduced MDLC step 1 at a high level. Today we went deep with a real example.

Seven steps to frame an ML problem.

  1. Step 1 Start with the business goal.
  2. Step 2 Turn it into a math target.
  3. Step 3 Define the end product.
  4. Step 4 Pick problem type.
  5. Step 5 Plan the data.
  6. Step 6 Set metrics.
  7. Step 7 Check assumptions.

Day 14 takeaways

  • Business goals like "increase revenue" must become clear ML targets like "reduce churn to 3.75%".
  • Frame the end product: predict who will leave, then act with offers or fixes.
  • Use classification for yes or no and regression for how likely someone is to leave.
  • Plan data, metrics, batch or online learning, and assumptions before you train.

Good framing is step 1 of MDLC. Get this right and the rest of the project becomes much clearer.
